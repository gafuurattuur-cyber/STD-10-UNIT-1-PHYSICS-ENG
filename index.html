Oscillation & Amplitude

Oscillation is a periodic motion in which an object moves to and fro at regular intervals of time about its equilibrium position.

Amplitude (a) = Maximum displacement from the equilibrium position to one side.
SI Unit: metre (m)

A complete oscillation occurs when the body returns to its initial position in the same direction from where it started.

Example: A swing starting from O → A → B → O completes one oscillation.

Period & Frequency

Period (T): Time required to complete one oscillation. Unit: second (s)

Frequency (f): Number of oscillations per second. Unit: hertz (Hz)

f = 1 / T     T = 1 / f

1 kHz = 10³ Hz    1 MHz = 10⁶ Hz

When length of a pendulum increases, its frequency decreases.

Forced Vibration & Resonance

Forced Vibration: Vibration of an object induced by an external vibrating object.

Resonance: When the natural frequency of the forcing object equals the natural frequency of the forced object, the objects are said to be in resonance. They vibrate with maximum amplitude.

Natural frequency depends on: length, size, elasticity and nature of material of the object.

Example: Two hacksaw blades of same length vibrate with maximum amplitude when one is excited (resonance).

Wave Motion

Wave motion is the continuous transfer of energy through the oscillations of the particles of a medium.

Mechanical Waves: Require a medium (sound, water waves)

Electromagnetic Waves: Do not require a medium (light, radio waves)

Sound is a mechanical wave that needs a medium to travel.

Wave Characteristics

Wave speed v = f × λ
(frequency × wavelength)

Wavelength (λ): Distance between two consecutive crests / compressions. Unit: metre (m)

Amplitude determines loudness; Frequency determines pitch.

Reflection of Sound

Sound waves reflect from hard surfaces. Smooth surfaces give better reflection.

Applications: SONAR, Stethoscope, Ceiling design in halls, Sound boxes, Echo.

Echo is heard when the reflected sound reaches the ear after at least 0.1 s (minimum distance ~17 m).

Ultrasonic Waves & Applications

Human audible range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz)

Infrasonic: Frequency below 20 Hz

Ultrasonic: Frequency above 20 kHz

Applications of Ultrasonic waves: SONAR, Medical imaging (ultrasound), Cleaning, Detecting cracks in metals, Pest control.

Characteristics of Sound

Loudness depends on amplitude.

Pitch depends on frequency.

Quality / Timbre depends on the waveform (overtones).

Different musical instruments produce different quality of sound even at same pitch and loudness.

Types of Waves

Natural Frequency

Longitudinal Waves

In longitudinal waves, particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation.

Sound waves in air are longitudinal waves consisting of compressions and rarefactions.

Example: Sound, waves on a spring pushed and pulled.

Transverse Waves

In transverse waves, particles of the medium vibrate perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation.

Example: Waves on a string, light waves, water surface waves.

Wave Equation v = fλ

v = f × λ

If frequency doubles and wavelength halves, speed remains the same (in same medium).

Speed of sound in air ≈ 340 m/s at 25°C.

Applications

  • SONAR – uses ultrasonic waves to find depth of sea / detect objects.
  • Ultrasound scanning – medical imaging of internal organs.
  • Stethoscope – uses multiple reflections of sound.
  • Echo – used to measure distance.

True / False Logic

Why does this happen?

Q: Why does a mixie make the table vibrate?

A: Forced vibration – the table is forced to vibrate by the mixie.

Q: Why do two identical tuning forks sound louder when one is excited and stem pressed on table?

A: Resonance – table + fork system vibrates with larger amplitude.
